quincunx

Discussing how the introductory course on mechanics ought to implement numerics, Karsten and I were perhaps somehwat in disagreement. I knew that the second mandatory assignment the students had to complete (which was something to the effect of particle image velocimetry interface recognition) was almost universally hated by the students. First of all, this assignment barely counted as numerical work. Second, this assignment evidently did not do its job in terms of getting students interested in mechanics as a subject. In my opinion, the main problem mechanics has at the Department of Mathematics at the University of Oslo is that the students fail to recognize the mathematics of it all. The courses at the graduate level are all courses in partial differential equations and methods thereof, but this is completely lost on the students that take the introductory course. Karsten would go on to somewhat overhaul the curriculum, and in my opinion improve the course overall. Still, I felt the course could improve by the implementation of some numerical work. Karsten told me he had an idea for an assignment on modeling the trade winds as sinusoidals along lines of longitude (north-south). I told him about the second mandatory assignment in Mikael's course Numerical Methods for Partial Differential Equations, where the students are given some skeleton code, which they have to fill in. This course is almost universally loved by the students, and I had found the assignment very fun. Karsten was hesitant, so I told him I could implement it for him, and he could do what he wanted with it.

And so I went on thinking of interesting ways to implement this in a way that would be interesting for the students, and make them realize mechanics opens the door for a lot of very interesting math. Having read all the classics, I thought a complex analysis approach to fluid mechanics would serve as a quaint such introduction. I went looking for conformal transformations of the globe (that wasn't necessarily the Mercator projection), for I knew of Riemann's stereographic projection. Being able to visualize the trade winds on a flat map could also perhaps inspire the students to be a little creative. Looking through the Wikipedia entry for map projections, I found the perfect candidate: Peirce's quincuncial projection.

Soon enough, the time came where I had to travel to Svalbard, and I got too busy with my other obligations, and thus the project fell through. I had only made a partial implementation in Python at that point, and Karsten had made the assignment all by himself. In the time since, I have thought about this little project of mine periodically, and I have now finally decided to complete it. Since I don't have any reason to make a map of the world now though, I figured I would make a map of the night sky, choosing to transform the constellations instead of the continents.
